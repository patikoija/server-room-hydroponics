# 05 — Reservoir pH (Atlas Scientific EZO-pH)

Continuous reservoir pH, reading over I²C on the same bus the input expanders use.

**Bought as the [Atlas pH Kit](https://atlas-scientific.com/kits/ph-kit/), $174.99**, which
bundles everything: EZO-pH circuit, Gen 3 lab-grade double-junction probe, an **electrically
isolated EZO carrier board**, six 20 ml calibration pouches (two each of pH 4 / 7 / 10) and a
20 ml **storage solution** pouch.

> Buying the circuit ($45.99) and probe (~$170) separately comes to **$215.99 with no isolator,
> no buffers and no storage solution**. The kit is **$41 cheaper and complete**. See
> [02 — BOM](02-bom.md#buy-the-kit-not-the-pieces).

## It connects over I²C, not as an analog input

Worth stating plainly because it tripped us up once. The controller has
[no analog inputs at all](03-controller.md#it-has-no-analog-inputs-none), and a bare pH electrode
(±414 mV at ~100 MΩ) cannot be read by any general-purpose ADC anyway.

**The EZO circuit is the bridge.** Probe → EZO-pH → I²C → the bus already running the PCF8574
input expanders at 0x21–0x26. The EZO-pH lands on **0x63**, so no collision, and ESPHome has an
official `ezo` platform.

## Stamp orientation on the carrier

The EZO module has two rows of three pins and is **not keyed** — it will physically seat either
way round.

**The rule: the probe pins face the probe.** `PRB` and `PGND` are the electrode connections and
route to the BNC, so that row must sit on the **BNC side**. `GND / TX / RX` faces the 5-pin
header. Seated correctly, the "pH EZO™" silkscreen ends up bottom-left, reading right-way-up with
the header at the top.

| Carrier 5-pin header | Wire to |
|---|---|
| **VCC** | **3.3 V — not 5 V.** The circuit itself takes 3.3–5.5 V, but the host side of the isolator takes its logic level from whatever you feed it, and 5 V I²C into a 3.3 V ESP32 is how you kill the ESP32. |
| **OFF** | leave unconnected |
| **GND** | GND |
| **TX** | **SDA** |
| **RX** | **SCL** |

> **Do not solder it.** Page 4 of the datasheet is a full-page warning: soldering the device voids
> the warranty. It press-fits into the female headers on the carrier.

## It ships in UART mode, and that looks exactly like a dead kit

An unswitched circuit is **invisible to an I²C scan**. Indistinguishable from a $175 kit that
arrived broken. The datasheet procedure:

1. Disconnect ground (power off)
2. Disconnect TX and RX
3. **Connect TX to PGND**
4. Confirm RX is disconnected
5. Connect ground (power on)
6. **Wait for the LED to change from green to blue**
7. Disconnect ground (power off)
8. Reconnect all data and power

> Manually switching to I²C sets the address to **99 (0x63)**.

**Do this on the unseated stamp.** `TX` and `PGND` are on *opposite rows* of the module, and the
carrier's own header is `VCC / OFF / GND / TX / RX` — it exposes **no `PGND` at all**, so there is
no carrier-pin shortcut. Lift the stamp out of its socket, short `TX → PGND` across the two rows,
power-cycle per the steps above, then re-seat it with the probe pins facing the probe.

This is the procedure that actually worked, after an earlier note claimed the short was "easier
done on the carrier's own pins", which is impossible.

## Commissioning tells

From the datasheet, describing this rig exactly:

> the pH circuit can read micro-voltages bleeding into the water from unnatural sources such as
> **pumps, solenoid valves**, or other probes/sensors … it is common to see **rapidly fluctuating
> readings or readings that are pinned to 14 or 0**.

So **0 or 14 is electrical noise, not a dead probe.** Atlas's own test: put the probe in a cup of
water on its own — it should settle immediately. The isolated carrier is the fix and it is already
in the kit.

The software treats 0 and 14 as noise rather than data, which matters: a reading of 14 that is
allowed into a trend looks like a catastrophic pH excursion.

## Probe care and the real running cost

**The probe lives in storage solution whenever it is not in the reservoir.** A dried glass bulb
is an irreversible failure — the fastest way to kill one of these, and not recoverable by
reconditioning.

The probe is the only part that wears. The circuit, the isolated carrier and the buffer pouches
are all reusable, so a replacement is **the probe alone, not another kit**.

| | Lab Grade `ENV-40-pH` (what this uses) | Industrial `ENV-50-pH` |
|---|---|---|
| Price | **$99.99** | $319.99 |
| Life, continuously submerged | ~2.5 years + | ~4 years + |
| **Running cost** | **≈ $40/year** | ≈ $80/year |
| Junction | double, Tyvek — protects the reference electrode in protein/salt-rich solution | Teflon flat-surface, aimed at wine tanks and inline pipe fittings |

**Do not "upgrade" to the industrial probe.** It is twice the running cost for a design aimed at a
different problem; the lab-grade double junction is both correct for a nutrient reservoir and
cheaper per year.

### Recalibration cadence: about once a year, not monthly

> This page used to say "budget recalibration every 30–60 days". That is **consumer-grade
> guidance applied to a lab-grade probe** — roughly **12× too often**.

Over-calibrating is not harmless. Every calibration is a fresh opportunity to introduce technique
error (unsettled readings, buffer cross-contamination) that later shows up as a low slope figure
nobody can explain.

A hydroponic reservoir is a mild environment — weak acids and bases — so drift is slow.
**~Once a year for the first two years, then ~every six months.** Recalibrate off-schedule only
when a reading looks wrong against a fresh buffer, or when the slope reading says the probe is
out of band.

**The glass is not replaceable, but a tired probe is often recoverable.** The body is a sealed
assembly, so a cracked bulb means a new probe — but short of that, Atlas sell a reconditioning
kit (the procedure opens with a ~15 s dip in 0.1N HCl). Two weeks in a Masterblend solution can
deposit a mineral film that reads as a degraded probe and simply cleans off. **Try reconditioning
first.**

## Trusting the reading before acting on it

A drifted probe reports a plausible number forever. That is the same failure class as a stuck
float, except it kills the crop slowly instead of obviously — so nothing was wired to dosing
until the reading could be *qualified*.

Three independent checks are ANDed into a single "dosing permitted" signal, all failing closed:

1. **Reading sanity** — 0 and 14 are noise per the datasheet, not readings.
2. **Calibration staleness** — how long since the last calibration, parsed out of the circuit's
   own calibration response.
3. **Probe slope** — the acid and base slope percentages the circuit reports. A healthy probe
   reads ~95–105% on both. **This is the only one of the three that can see a probe dying despite
   a fresh calibration**, which is exactly the failure a days-since-calibration counter cannot
   detect.

## Known trade-off: one failure domain

The pH circuit shares the I²C bus with the input expanders. I²C is shared, and **a wedged device
holds SDA low** — so the pH circuit and the overflow-failsafe float now sit in **one failure
domain**, which is what a second ESP32 node would have avoided.

That trade was taken by default rather than decided. It is cheap to revisit, and the trigger to
revisit it is the first unexplained bus error on this node.

For the same reason, when a dosing pump arrives it goes on a **separate** node — a 12 V
peristaltic at 45 ml/min, with pH Down diluted 1:10 so a correction is a 13-second run instead of
a 1.3-second one. A stuck relay then delivers 10× less acid before anybody notices.

## Observed behaviour

Reservoir pH drifts **up about 0.5 over three days** on the 60-gallon reservoir with a crop on
it. That is real drift in the expected direction as the crop draws nutrient down — not noise, and
the reason this wants a trend alert rather than a spot check.
